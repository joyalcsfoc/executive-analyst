Each rung scores higher than the one below, but the steps are not the same size. The semantic layer adds 13 points, all of it from picking the right word on Question 1 and pricing the answer on Question 2. It adds nothing to completeness. The ontology and graph add 47 points, almost all of it completeness. Two different problems, two different layers. Neither does the other's job.

01

What actually changed between the three

All three are published Genie Agent spaces on gold_dev_analytics, and none can read a table we did not hand it. Two things change across the rungs: whether we certified the measures, and whether we declared the relationships.

1 · Tables only 2 · + Semantic layer 3 · + Ontology & KG

Descriptions & synonyms None None Every table, every column; 3 synonyms per key column
Text instructions None 1 block — routing, clarification, empty-period, formatting The same block + generated ontology grounding

SQL functions None None kg_find_node, kg_neighbors
Sourcing relation reachable ✗ dim_supplier_contract not exposed ✗ dim_supplier_contract not exposed ✓ as typed edges in kg_edges
Typical failure Answers one reading of the question, then says no other reading existed Correct maths over an incomplete list, every time Answers narrowly, but says what it measured

The semantic layer bought us nothing on the relationship question. Five certified metric views are the only difference between Setup 1 and Setup 2. Setup 2 still came back with the same incomplete part list, using about twice as many queries to get there. A semantic layer certifies how we calculate a number along a path that already exists. It cannot build a new path, and it cannot tell us we took the wrong one.

What Setup 3 adds is the ontology, and the ontology is what reaches the relationship. Neither space exposes supply_chain_analytics.dim_supplier_contract, the table that actually records who supplies what — it is not one of the 15 dimensions, and no query either space writes can touch it. Setup 3 has instead a declared relationship, mfg:supplierSuppliesPart, materialised into kg_edges and read through two functions. Those edges were built from dim_supplier_contract at materialisation time, so Setup 3 does reach a fact Setup 2 cannot — but only because we said in advance that the relationship exists and was worth carrying. That is the whole difference: not a bigger catalog, a declared one.

02

Question 1 — an ambiguous word

Q1
“What did we spend last month?”

The maths is easy, and that is the point. Our data holds at least three unrelated things a C-level user might call “spend”: campaign media spend, cost of poor quality, and discount given away. The ontology declares four meanings — ea:Cost disambiguatesTo ea:CampaignSpend, ea:CostPerLead, ea:COPQ, ea:Discount — and the numbers are nowhere near each other in size. The agent can calculate any of them perfectly and still hand us the wrong answer. “Last month” hides a second trap: our domains do not end on the same date, so the agent has to work it out per domain rather than read it off a calendar.

Verdict · Question 1

The real gap is between Setup 1 and the other two, and one instruction block buys it — not the ontology. Setup 1 calculated nothing badly. It calculated the wrong thing with full confidence, then took away our reason to check by saying nothing else existed. One page of clarification text prevents that, and it is worth knowing before you buy an ontology.

Setups 2 and 3 make the same call, so the difference is whether we can audit it. We expanded Setup 2's thinking to find out: it is one bullet repeating its own answer. Setup 3's trace names the ambiguity, names the three candidate measures, and commits up front to a period rule. Both refuse to guess; only one shows us why. On a single question that hardly matters. Across a standing Benchmark set, it is the difference between a regression we can diagnose and one we can only watch.

Neither offered cost per lead, and the obvious explanation is wrong — our compile path carries all four meanings into the space instructions. Offering three is a fair reading of the word “spend”, since cost per lead is a ratio, not an amount spent.

03

Question 2 — a supplier blast radius

Q2
“If Supplier 001 goes down tomorrow, which warehouses and parts are exposed?”

This question asks the agent to follow a relationship, and there is no join it can use to do it. We gave dim_supplier_contract — one row per contracted (supplier, part) pair — to none of the three spaces. For Setups 1 and 2, the only column sourcing and inventory share is PART_KEY, sitting on two facts that count different things. So the obvious move is to link supplier to part through fact_supplier_quality — an inspection log. That is not a broken join. It is a narrower relationship wearing the right name: “supplies this part and we have inspected it”.

Verdict · Question 2

The two lower setups tie at 3 of 9, and how they got there matters more than the tie. Setup 1 classified the parts and never priced them. Setup 2 priced them to the rupee and never classified them. Neither found the fourth part, the seventh warehouse, or the third at-risk location, and neither told us what date it was looking at. Both presented their totals as totals.

This is not the model being smarter, and the matching sources prove it. Setups 2 and 3 got the same 25 tables, and neither holds dim_supplier_contract, so no prompt will ever get Setup 2 to the fourth part. That ceiling is built in. The fix is a deployment, not a prompt.

Setup 3's single loss is the fair counterweight, and it flips the ranking on the one thing an executive is most likely to ask next. It gave us the complete picture and no money. Setup 2 gave us exact money over an incomplete picture. Of those two faults, the missing rupee column is the one we can actually see.

04

Why the graph found the fourth part

This is the whole report in one comparison, and it has nothing to do with how good the model is.

Two tables can tell you who supplies a part. The contract register lists every part a supplier has agreed to supply. That is the real answer, and it holds 60 rows. The inspection log lists every part somebody has checked the quality of. It looks like the same thing and holds 45 rows. Neither table's name warns you about the difference.

The lower two setups never had the contract register, so the inspection log was their only route. They took it and lost a quarter of the answer — 3 of 4 parts, 75%, which is exactly the ratio the inspection route yields across the whole catalog. We build the graph from the contract register on purpose, because we already hit this same bug once, when we built the graph the wrong way round: 

-- src/ontology/edge_sources.sql   (hand-written, runs before materialize_kg)
--
-- Contract-derived, not event-derived. The previous source for supplierSuppliesPart
-- was fact_supplier_quality — an inspection-grain fact — which silently narrowed the
-- relation to "supplies this part AND we have inspected it": 45 of 60 contracted
-- parts. Parts never inspected (e.g. Blower Motor P0071, Headrest Guide P0088) had a
-- preferred supplier on contract but no edge in the graph, so traversals reported
-- them as unsourced.
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW ontology.part_supplier_sourcing AS
  SELECT s.SUPPLIER_KEY, p.PART_KEY, c.is_preferred
  FROM        supply_chain_analytics.dim_supplier_contract c
  JOIN        dim.dim_supplier s ON s.SUPPLIER_ID = c.supplier_id
  JOIN        dim.dim_part     p ON p.PART_ID     = c.part_id;

-- Guard: every contract row must resolve to both surrogate keys, or fail the run.
-- assert_kg_integrity's empty_rel check only fires at zero edges, so it would not
-- notice this relation quietly shrinking again — exactly how the original defect survived.

That guard exists because this same relationship already shrank once and nobody noticed. Setups 1 and 2 reproduce that exact bug live in this study. The check is the difference between a bug we fix once and a bug that returns on the next dimension reload.

So the ontology does not make the agent cleverer. It means somebody wrote down, once, which of two similar-looking tables is the real one — and now the agent cannot pick the other. On Question 2 that was two function calls: kg_find_node('Supplier 001'), then kg_neighbors('Supplier', key, 'supplierSuppliesPart'). Following the graph replaced all the hunting.

Three layers, three kinds of knowledge

Layer In one line The question it answers for the agent What goes wrong without it
Semantic layer A dictionary of numbers “When someone says inventory value, which column do I add up, and what am I not allowed to add it across?” Every answer works out the maths from scratch. Ask twice, get two different numbers, and nothing tells you a choice was made.
Ontology A dictionary of words “Does depot mean warehouse? Does spend have more than one meaning? Is at risk three bands or four?” The agent picks one meaning of your word and answers that perfectly. You never find out it chose.
Knowledge graph A map of connections “Which supplier feeds this part? Which warehouses hold it? Can I walk from one to the other without guessing a join?” The agent joins whichever two tables share a column. The answer is right about the rows it found, and missing the ones it never found.

We maintain one of the three by hand. exec_analyst.ttl is 743 lines; from it generate.py writes the graph DDL, the two lookup functions, seven data assertions, the glossary tags, the disambiguation context and the space config — eight artifacts, each with a do not edit by hand header, and the build fails if any is older than the TTL.

The short version

The semantic layer stops the agent calculating the number wrong. The ontology stops it answering the wrong question. The graph stops it answering only part of one. Here the first was worth 13 points and the other two together were worth 47 — and only the third caught a high-risk part that was about to run out.

06

Recommendation

Build on Setup 3

Genie Agent with the semantic layer, the ontology and the knowledge graph — and close its one gap, which is a two-line instruction change.

Setup 3 did not win everything. Setup 2 was the only session that priced the exposure, and when a supplier goes down, money is not optional. Setup 3 is the only one whose remaining fault you can see. A missing rupee column makes you ask a follow-up question. A missing part does not.

Three things earn it the recommendation:

  1. Only the graph answered in full. Both lower setups gave three of four contracted parts, six of seven exposed warehouses and two of three locations at immediate risk — and presented each as a complete count. That ceiling is built into the setup, not the behaviour: neither space can see dim_supplier_contract, so no prompt will raise it.
  2. Declared relationships stop the thrashing. Setup 2 spent six repair rounds and about seventeen steps poking at tables that were never a sourcing register, and still came back short. Setup 3 followed one edge, took eleven steps, and fixed nothing. That saves warehouse time as well as improving accuracy.
  3. Only the ontology gave us an answer we can audit. Named definitions on Question 1, a stated snapshot date and classified parts on Question 2. We cannot regression-test an answer that will not say what it measured, or when.

Do this next

  1. Spell out “follow the graph, then measure” in the space instructions. The one checkpoint Setup 3 lost is a routing gap, not a data gap. Once it has the entity set from kg_edges, it should price them with MEASURE(total_stock_valuation) on metrics_inventory_snapshot before answering. Do not put money into kg_edges — keeping it navigation-only is what keeps those two tables small and correct.
  2. Put both questions in Benchmark, with their counts as assertions. “4 parts, 7 warehouses, 3 at-risk locations, snapshot 2026-09-04” is exactly the kind of claim that breaks without anyone seeing it. A standing set of questions turns this evaluation into a test.
  3. Decide whether cost per lead belongs in the clarification — accept three options for “spend” and assert that, or split the instruction so “cost” offers all four. Pick one and write it down. Leaving it open is what will make the next run look like a regression.
  4. Add a supplier → warehouse relationship, or approve the two-hop. The agent works out the reach of an outage by following supplierSuppliesPart then partStockedAt, every single time. It is the question we get asked most, and it deserves to be one documented path.
  5. Do not put the tables-only space in front of an executive. It is a decent exploration tool for someone who knows the schema and will check the output. Here it closed one question by claiming the data was not there, and reported a partial answer as a total — with no reason for the reader to doubt either.

One caveat on the scores. Our structural claims rest on the definitions in the repo and on Setup 3's answer containing everything the other two returned plus more. We have not independently re-run SQL to certify the raw counts in this pass. The full report carries five queries that settle them in one run; the decisive one is whether the inspection route returns 3 parts where the contract register returns 4.
